Twilight observing with Keck/SCALES

Right after sunset and right before sunrise, the brightening sky makes observing impossible in the optical – but the infrared sky brightness remains (mostly) unchanged. Infrared instruments like SCALES can take advantage of often-underutilized twilight time to do high-impact science.

SCALES: a new thermal-infrared integral field spectrograph for Keck Observatory

SCALES in the UCSC cleanroom. Googly eyes courtesy of Phil Hinz.

The Slicer Combined with an Array of Lenslets for Exoplanet Spectroscopy (SCALES) instrument is a thermal infrared, AO-fed, high-contrast integral field spectrograph nearing completion for Keck Observatory, with commissioning expected for early 2027.

SCALES is comprised of a 1-5μm 12.3”x 12.3”imaging channel designed to replicate NIRC2’s capabilities, and a 2-5μm integral field spectrograph with low resolution (R∼35-200) and medium resolution (R∼2500-5000) modes, offering new capabilities compared to existing Keck instrumentation. The low-resolution IFU uses a lenslet-based design, and the medium-resolution IFU using a novel combined slicer and lenslet array (“slenslit”) design (discussed in Stelter et al. 2026).

SCALES is optimized for exoplanet high-contrast imaging, but will also be a workhorse instrument for a broad range of science cases at Keck, including protoplanet characterization, protoplanetary disk mapping, Solar System object monitoring, and characterization of supernova remnants, active galactic nuclei, and more. Operating at longer wavelengths than existing near-infrared high-contrast IFUs (e.g. GPI, CHARIS, SPHERE), SCALES is sensitive to older and colder exoplanets than have previously been directly imaged from the ground.

Outreach and committment to equity

Lack of support for students at every transition through the education system – K12 to college, college to grad school – produces cumulative disadvantages, diverting otherwise brilliant and capable people both from becoming professional scientists, and from becoming curious, scientifically literate lifelong learners outside of professional science. I am committed to intervening in every stage of the leaky pipeline, from K12 STEM education to the development of professional scientists.

K12 astronomy and instrumentation outreach

Equity begins with early access. Students often decide whether STEM is "for them" well before they apply to college, and engaging K12 students in STEM and astronomy-specific outreach has the potential to intervene in self-minimizing beliefs such as "I'm not smart enough for this" or "this isn't for people like me." I have organized ~15 classroom visits and student tours of lab facilities over the past 6 years, both through collaborations with local public schools and through programs like Skype a Scientist.

I am currently working with the University of California Observatories to establish regular public school and community college tours of the UCO Astronomy Shops on the UCSC campus. I developed the first iteration of this program in Spring 2026. We brought the ~85 2nd graders from Arroyo Seco Academy to the shops, where professional astronomers and engineers introduced them to astronomy and astronomical instrumentation through grade-appropriate discussions and demonstrations.

Funding early-career astronomers with the Osterbrock Rising Graduate Award

The accumulated financial obstacles to attaining higher education have a clear impact on the field: the socioeconomic makeup of institutions of higher education has static for the past 70 years (Abramitzky et al. 2024). Simply applying to graduate school can be prohibitively expensive, with students often spending upwards of $1,200.

I co-founded the Osterbrock Rising Graduate Award program to offset the cost of graduate school applications for UCSC undergraduates applying to STEM graduate programs. With support from the Osterbrock Leadership Program, we have supported 18 UCSC undergraduate students who are applying to graduate programs in various STEM disciplines. Each student receives a $500 award and meets with program mentors to advise students in their graduate application process and help refine their application materials. Past awardees have gone on to physics, astrophysics, geophysics, and biology Masters and PhD programs across the US, Canada, and UK.
